A Gentleman's Game is Tom Coyne's debut novel, published in 2001 and adapted the following year into a little-seen film of the same name. (An unrelated Greg Rucka spy thriller shares the title; the golf book is what is meant here.) The story follows Timmy Price, a boy of about thirteen whose father, a member of an exclusive Delaware country club, installs him as a caddie there partly as a lesson in humility. Timmy proves a natural at the game and an uneasy witness to the men who play it: the members whose conduct is the antithesis of the honor they claim as they lie, steal, and cheat at the sport they call a gentleman's game. The book's darkest thread runs beneath that hypocrisy—a working-class caddie, Jamie, is molested by a powerful member who then leverages the club's stocking of judges, a district attorney, and a congressman to keep it buried. The title is ironic by design, and that irony is the whole engine of the thing: golf's etiquette mythology set against the ungentlemanly people who invoke it.

On release the novel drew warm if modest trade attention—Publishers Weekly called it an appealing first effort and a satisfyingly idiosyncratic coming-of-age story about class, family, and the fickleness of the sport, while also flagging a real structural flaw: Timmy's violent, antisocial brother is set up early and never pays off. Coyne's own publicity reports that the Philadelphia Daily News named it one of the twenty-five best sports books of all time, though that is a self-supplied accolade I cannot independently corroborate and would treat cautiously. What is clearer is that the book no longer occupies much space in the conversation. Coyne built his reputation elsewhere, on the first-person golf travelogues A Course Called Ireland, Scotland, and America, and the quest memoir Paper Tiger, and in January 2024 became editor of the quarterly The Golfer's Journal. Against that body of work the early fiction reads as a road not taken; whether the novel remains in print I could not confirm.

The film is the more visible and the more abused artifact of the two. Directed by J. Mills Goodloe from a screenplay he and Coyne adapted, it starred Mason Gamble, Dylan Baker, Philip Baker Hall, and Gary Sinise, and was made with notable autobiographical intimacy. Timmy's house is Coyne's parents' house, where he was living during filming, on grounds where he had once caddied. It surfaced largely as a direct-to-video release and has been treated unkindly since: a Golf Digest writer revisited it in 2016 framing it as, by his account, the worst golf movie ever made. That verdict is hyperbole and not universal; it carries roughly a 6.3 user average on IMDb, and some viewers defend it as a Stand by Me–style coming-of-age piece rather than a sports film. The recurring and more credible complaint is structural: too many subplots, a main story that gets lost, threads introduced and left to wither. The molestation material also earned the picture an R rating that sits oddly against its otherwise earnest, almost family-uplift register.

The context that produced both is worth naming, because it explains the ambition and the unevenness alike. The novel arrived at the tail of a turn-of-the-millennium vogue for golf as moral and even mystical uplift on screen (Tin Cup in 1996, The Legend of Bagger Vance in 2000), and it reads partly as a corrective to that reverence, sour about the country-club class where those films were dewy-eyed about the game's soul. Caddyshack is the more honest ancestor, and the film acknowledges as much by casting Brian Doyle-Murray, who ran the caddie shack in the 1980 original. A young writer drawing directly on his own loop at a Philadelphia-area club gives the caddie world real texture; that specificity is the work's genuine strength, along with the central conceit and Sinise's restrained turn as the burnt-out former amateur champion.

The limits are equally specific and not redeemed by good intentions. The plot is overstuffed with an abuse cover-up, a reclusive mentor, a withholding father and an unused violent brother, so no single line carries enough weight, a diffuseness the film inherits and worsens. The moral architecture is schematic: the decent poor boy against corrupt rich men, with the title's irony applied so heavily it stops being irony and becomes thesis. As for where it is heading, the honest answer is nowhere in particular. Its durable function is as the origin point in Coyne's biography and as occasional fodder for the worst-movie-ever genre of rediscovery. That is a modest afterlife, and on the evidence a fair one: the conceit was sharper and more honest than its era's reverent golf pictures, but the execution—in print and especially on film—never found the discipline to make the indictment land.
