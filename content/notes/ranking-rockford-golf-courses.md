Publicly playable golf courses within 50 straight-line miles of Rockford, Michigan, evaluated against Alister MacKenzie's 13 principles of golf-course architecture.

For this survey, "publicly playable" means an ordinary golfer can obtain a tee time without private-club membership. The initial ranking concentrates on regulation 18-hole courses. Nine-hole-only and short courses should be considered separately.

## Scoring

Each of MacKenzie's principles receives:

- **1**—strongly embodies the principle
- **0.5**—partially or inconsistently embodies it
- **0**—substantially conflicts with it

Maximum score: **13**

Principle 13, concerning a course being "equally good during winter and summer," is interpreted for Michigan as maintaining consistently good playing surfaces throughout the normal golf season.

## MacKenzie's 13 Principles

1. The course should be arranged in two loops of nine holes.
2. There should be a large proportion of good two-shot holes, two or three drive-and-pitch holes, and at least four one-shot holes.
3. There should be little walking between greens and tees.
4. The greens and fairways should be sufficiently undulating, but there should be no hill climbing.
5. Every hole should have a distinctive character.
6. Approaches should generally be visible, with few blind shots.
7. The course should possess beautiful surroundings and artificial features should harmonize with the natural environment.
8. There should be heroic carries from the tee, but always an alternative route for golfers unable to make them.
9. There should be infinite variety in the strokes required to play the various holes.
10. There should be no need to search excessively for lost balls.
11. The course should remain interesting to the scratch golfer even after repeated play.
12. The course should be enjoyable for the weaker golfer while presenting increasing difficulty as the player attempts better scores.
13. The course should maintain consistently good playing conditions throughout its normal season.

## The Leaders

### 1. Diamond Springs—12.5/13

Diamond Springs is the clearest expression of MacKenzie's philosophy in the Rockford region despite having no direct connection to MacKenzie.

Mike DeVries and Kris Shumaker routed the course across natural rises, eskers and a ravine with remarkably little artificial construction. Natural terrain determines the strategy. Fairways provide room while making position matter. Approaches vary substantially. The course is easily walked, and its restrained maintenance complements rather than overwhelms the architecture.

Most importantly, difficulty comes primarily from choices rather than punishment.

Diamond Springs sets the provisional benchmark for the region.

### 2. Pilgrim's Run—12.0/13

Pilgrim's Run combines excellent natural terrain with unusual variety.

Its origin—individual holes conceived by several amateur golfers and ultimately brought together by Mike DeVries and Kris Shumaker—might have produced an incoherent collection. Instead, the course possesses considerable individuality while retaining a coherent naturalistic character.

Wide enough to accommodate ordinary golfers, it nevertheless rewards thoughtful positioning and repeatedly changes the shots demanded of accomplished players.

### 3. American Dunes—11.5/13

American Dunes performs surprisingly well under a MacKenzie test.

The Nicklaus redesign opened the property, exposed its sandy ground and created broad playing corridors. Players repeatedly choose among lines rather than merely attempting to hit narrow predetermined targets.

The course also provides excellent variation in green size, orientation and approach requirements.

Its principal weakness is severity. Some green complexes and recovery situations move beyond strategic difficulty toward punishment, particularly for weaker players.

### 4. Mines Golf Club—11.0/13

The Mines demonstrates Mike DeVries's ability to find golf in unusual terrain.

Approximately 110 feet of elevation change and remnants of the property's mining history produce considerable ground movement and individuality without requiring the course to become artificially spectacular.

Its most important MacKenzie weakness is blindness. Several shots obscure their destination or landing area. MacKenzie explicitly opposed excessive blindness, particularly on approaches.

Otherwise, Mines scores remarkably well.

### 5. The Meadows—10.5/13

The Meadows at Grand Valley State University is particularly strong in width, walking, alternative routes and accommodation of different abilities.

The relatively open landscape gives players room to choose lines, while greens and hazards make those choices consequential.

Wetlands occasionally produce forced situations in which strategic optionality disappears, preventing the course from joining the very top group.

### 6. Tullymore—10.0/13

Tullymore demonstrates why a MacKenzie ranking differs from a conventional "best courses" ranking.

Jim Engh created abundant strategic choices, dramatic bunkering, highly distinctive holes and extraordinary variety. Better players encounter fascinating problems throughout the round.

The same architecture can become burdensome for weaker golfers. Wetlands, fescue, severe slopes and forced situations increase both punishment and the likelihood of lost balls.

MacKenzie wanted heroic golf, but his heroism was voluntary: the golfer should be able to attempt the dangerous carry for advantage or take a longer safe route.

Tullymore does not always provide that escape.

## Architectural Families

The Rockford radius reveals three broad architectural families.

The first is the **naturalistic and strategic tradition**, represented particularly strongly by:

- Diamond Springs
- Pilgrim's Run
- Mines
- Boulder Creek

These courses generally allow the land to dictate the golf. Width, angles, ground contours and greens provide much of the difficulty.

The second is **modern championship and resort architecture**, represented by:

- American Dunes
- Tullymore
- St. Ives
- Ravines
- Thornapple Pointe

These courses tend toward greater visual drama, more elaborate hazards and more overt difficulty. The best examples retain strategic alternatives; the weaker MacKenzie fits substitute forced execution for choice.

The third is **traditional community public golf**, represented by courses such as:

- Kaufman
- North Kent
- Links at Bowen Lake

These courses may lack spectacular architecture but sometimes satisfy MacKenzie surprisingly well because they are walkable, comprehensible, economical and enjoyable for golfers of widely different abilities.

## What the MacKenzie Test Changes

A MacKenzie ranking should not simply reproduce a conventional ranking of the area's "best" golf courses.

MacKenzie gives little credit for:

- expensive conditioning
- clubhouse amenities
- prestige
- tournament difficulty
- high slope ratings
- spectacular forced carries
- visual intimidation for its own sake

He rewards:

- natural terrain
- efficient routing
- walking
- strategic width
- alternative lines
- visible approaches
- varied shots
- recoverability
- distinctive holes
- increasing strategic difficulty for better players
- enjoyment for weaker players
- repeated-play interest

That distinction substantially rearranges the normal hierarchy.

A dramatic resort course can therefore rank below an inexpensive daily-fee course if the latter provides better choices, better walking, greater recoverability and more natural golf.

## Provisional Rockford Benchmark

**Diamond Springs Golf Course—12.5/13**

Diamond Springs becomes the benchmark against which subsequent locations will be compared.

The question as the survey moves outward is therefore not whether another region contains a more famous, expensive or difficult golf course.

It is whether another publicly playable course embodies MacKenzie's conception of ideal golf more completely.
