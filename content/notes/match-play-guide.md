Match play is the older game. Long before anyone counted total strokes against a field, two players walked out together and contested one hole at a time—win it, lose it, or halve it, then start fresh on the next tee. Stroke play came later, a bookkeeping convenience for sorting large fields and selling the modern tournament. If you want the game closer to its original orientation, you play match play: you against a person, not against a number.

This is the format C&RBGC favors, and it rewards a different temperament than medal golf. A blow-up hole does not follow you around—it costs you that hole and nothing more. The arithmetic is simpler, the pressure is more personal, and a round can turn on a single conceded putt.

## How the scoring works

You are not adding up strokes. You are counting holes won. Whoever takes fewer strokes on a hole wins that hole and goes "one up." A tied hole is "halved" and the score does not change. The running score is always stated as holes up against holes remaining: **1 up**, **3 up**, or **all square** (often written **AS**) when nobody leads.

Because each hole is its own contest, a disaster is contained. Make a nine on a par four and you lose one hole, exactly the same as losing it by a single stroke. This is why match play forgives aggression—the downside of a bold shot is capped.

## When the match ends

A match is over the moment one player leads by more holes than remain to be played. That produces the familiar two-number result: **3 and 2** means three holes up with only two left, so the match is closed out on the 16th green. A match that runs the full distance and is decided on the last hole is won **1 up**.

One term worth knowing: **dormie**. You are dormie when you lead by exactly the number of holes left—say, two up with two to play. You cannot lose from there; your opponent must win every remaining hole just to force a halved match (or, where the competition requires a winner, extra holes).

## Concessions—the Move stroke play does not have

At any time you may concede your opponent's next stroke, a hole, or the entire match. A conceded stroke is most often a short putt—"that's good"—which lets play move briskly and spares everyone the agony of a knee-knocker that does not matter. You may also concede a hole when you are clearly beaten on it, and pick up rather than hole out.

Two things to remember: a concession, once made, cannot be declined or withdrawn, and you never have to hole out in match play. Concede generously but deliberately—conceding a three-footer early can plant the thought that you will give the next one too, and then you don't.

## Order of play and the honour

The draw or agreement settles the honour on the first tee; after that, the winner of the previous hole tees off first. Through the green and on the putting surface, the player whose ball is farther from the hole—the player "away"—plays first.

Order matters more here than in stroke play because there is a remedy for getting it wrong: if your opponent plays out of turn, you may cancel the stroke and require them to replay it in the correct order. Most of the time you will simply play on, but the option is yours.

## Rules differences from stroke play

The biggest difference is the penalty currency. In stroke play almost everything costs strokes; in match play the **general penalty is loss of the hole**, not two strokes. A serious breach does not bloat your score—it simply hands that hole to your opponent.

A few other points follow from the head-to-head structure:

- You can pick up and concede a hole rather than holing out, so there is no "no return" to record.
- Disputes are handled on the spot. If you doubt a ruling, raise it before either player tees off on the next hole (or, on the 18th, before the result is final). Settle it then, not in the clubhouse.
- Giving wrong information about the number of strokes you have taken can cost you the hole, so answer honestly when asked.

## Handicaps

Singles match play normally uses the full difference in course handicaps. The lower handicapper plays from scratch within the match, and the higher handicapper receives that difference in strokes, allotted on the hardest holes first according to the card's stroke index. If you get six strokes, you get them on the six holes indexed 1 through 6, one apiece, and a net birdie there can steal a hole outright.

Handicap allowances vary by format and by what the committee sets—singles is typically played off the full difference, alternate-shot and better-ball formats off a reduced percentage. Check the allowance before you start so the strokes land where you expect.

## The formats

- **Singles**—one against one, the purest version.
- **Foursomes** (alternate shot)—two-player sides share one ball, alternating shots, with players alternating the tee on odd and even holes. Demanding and intimate; you live with your partner's misses and they with yours.
- **Four-ball** (better ball)—each of the four plays their own ball, and each side counts its lower score on the hole. More forgiving, because either partner can carry a hole.
- **Greensomes** and other hybrids—both partners drive, then choose one ball and alternate in; a gentler cousin of foursomes.[^1]

The Ryder Cup template—foursomes, four-ball, then singles—is the classic way to build a team event out of these pieces.

## Strategy and temperament

Match play is played against a person, so play the person. Watch what your opponent has left; their trouble changes your calculus. When they are in the trees, the smart shot is the safe one—make them beat your par. When they have stuffed it close, that is the moment to take on the flag, because a conceded hole costs the same whether you lose it by one or by five.

Momentum is real and the halved hole is underrated. Stopping a run of lost holes with a hard-won half can swing a match as much as winning one. Keep your own number off the scoreboard in your head; the only score that matters is holes up or down, and that resets your attention to the hole in front of you. Stay patient when you are down—matches turn fast, and dormie is closer than it feels.

## Etiquette

Concede putts at a reasonable pace and without theater. Announce the match score on the tee so there is never a dispute about where things stand. Keep moving—match play is meant to be brisk. And win or lose, the match closes with a handshake on the green where it ended. That handshake, more than the result, is the point.

A forgiving way to pair unequal players and still play golf to the hole—tolerated, even liked, but not the Club's first reach.
