A template hole is not a copy. It is a reusable strategic idea: a particular relationship among hazards, angles, contours, and what the golfer can see. C. B. Macdonald began the practice after studying the British holes he most admired, and Seth Raynor and Charles Banks carried it much further across the courses they built after him. Macdonald set out the thinking in _Scotland's Gift: Golf_ (1928), which the [hickory-era writing survey](/notes/hickory-era-golf-writing-survey/) counts among the foundational American books on the game.

There is no agreed closed list. A handful of forms are canonical, others turn up only now and then, and modern lists run from about a dozen core templates to more than twenty. What follows is the traditional family.

## The four par 3s

The most nearly canonical templates are four short holes, and Macdonald and Raynor used them as a deliberately varied set: a short precision hole, a medium iron, an angled running shot, and a long heroic one.

- **Redan.** A medium-to-long par 3 whose green sits diagonally to the line of play and slopes away and sideways, classically right to left, so that a ball landed short and right feeds onto it. From the 15th at North Berwick. A Reverse Redan mirrors the geometry, and is a variation rather than a separate template.
- **Short.** A short par 3 whose lack of length is answered by a heavily defended, often raised and boldly contoured green, frequently ringed with sand and sometimes marked with a thumbprint or other internal hollow. Inspired by Royal West Norfolk at Brancaster.
- **Eden.** A medium par 3 after the 11th on the Old Course: a strongly sloping green, usually back to front, defended by deep bunkers, where distance control and staying below the hole matter more than anything.
- **Biarritz.** Traditionally a long par 3 with an enormous green cut across by a deep swale, leaving front and rear plateaus. The ideal shot lands on the front and runs through the swale to the back.

## Problems from the tee

- **Cape.** A hazard runs diagonally across or beside the line of play, and the golfer chooses how much of it to bite off; more risk buys a shorter shot or a better angle. Macdonald appears to have originated it himself at National Golf Links.
- **Road.** A long par 4 or short par 5 after the Old Course's 17th. A bold tee line gives the best angle into a shallow diagonal green guarded by a deep front bunker, with trouble just beyond, usually a rear bunker standing in for the St Andrews road.
- **Leven.** A shorter par 4. The dangerous line from the tee carries a bunker, burn, or waste area and earns an open view of the green; the safe line leaves a partly blind approach over mounding.
- **Long.** A three-shot par 5 after the Old Course's 14th, where a great cross hazard in the manner of Hell Bunker forces a decision on the second shot: challenge it for position or lay up and accept a harder third.
- **Narrows.** A long two-shot hole where paired hazards pinch the route at the distance that matters, which Macdonald described as a composite of holes at North Berwick and Muirfield.
- **Bottle.** A corridor that narrows progressively or is split by centerline hazards. Stay short where there is room, take the safer and poorer side, or challenge the neck.
- **Sahara.** A shorter two-shot hole with a large, often blind sandy waste to cross or challenge from the tee.
- **Alps.** A substantial par 4 with a blind or nearly blind approach over a ridge, to a green that is often receptive enough to gather a well-judged unseen shot. From Prestwick's 17th.
- **Channel.** Typically a par 5 offering an alternate fairway, separated from the main route by sand or water, that shortens the hole for the golfer who takes the hazardous line. The famous version was at the original Lido.

## Problems at the green

- **Plateau** and **Double Plateau.** A green raised distinctly above its surroundings; in the double form, two raised platforms joined by a lower section, so the day's hole location decides which side of the fairway to approach from.
- **Punchbowl.** A green sunk in a hollow or ringed by banks that gather shots toward it, often partly hidden from the approach.
- **Lion's Mouth.** A deep bunker cut into the front center of the green, splitting the target into left and right; the flag decides the angle.
- **Maiden.** Raised sections divided by a substantial trough. Raynor's Maidens became their own idea, only loosely related to the original Maiden at Royal St George's.
- **Knoll.** A green set on a pronounced mound, with falloffs that reject an inaccurate approach.
- **Hog's Back.** A fairway along a convex ridge, where the crest gives the best position and anything off line kicks down one side.

A few more names, among them Valley, Prize Dogleg, Strategy, and Principal's Nose, appear at particular Macdonald and Raynor courses or in discussions of the Lido, but they belong to a second tier rather than the basic vocabulary.

## Why the Club cares

The names describe problems presented to the golfer, not shapes on a plan. A good Redan can look nothing like North Berwick; a Cape needs no water; a Leven can be built on entirely different ground. What carries from one version to the next is the proposition: take one line and gain an advantage, take another and accept a different problem. That is why the templates are architectural vocabulary rather than historical replicas, and why they fit the [design principles](/notes/golf-course-design-principles/) the Club holds to, beginning with a course that offers engaging puzzles to solve.

They suit bogey golf, too. Nearly every template keeps a safe line open; it simply charges for it. A golfer playing against [Colonel Bogey](/notes/colonel-bogey-and-the-bogey-competition/) can take the long way round the Cape or lay up short of the Long's cross hazard and still make the Colonel's number, which is the whole idea of a strategic hole: the hero and the walker play the same ground and face different questions.

The family is easier to visit than it used to be. Tom Doak's recreation of Macdonald's lost 1914 Lido opened at Sand Valley in 2022, and it heads the list of [Golden Age walking courses](/notes/golden-age-walking-courses-near-grand-rapids/); Doak and Jim Urbina's Old Macdonald at Bandon carries his name.
