The Club carries the bogeyman in its name, and golf knows him mostly as [Colonel Bogey](/notes/colonel-bogey-and-the-bogey-competition/), the phantom who plays bogey on every hole. But the Colonel is a late commission. Long before the Great Yarmouth golfers borrowed him around 1890, the bogeyman lived in the nursery, and what he was there explains why the borrowing worked.

## A word with no root

The Colonel's own note traces the word back to Middle English _bugge_ and sets aside the popular origin stories. Two neighbors belong to the same family. _Bugbear_ (1570s) was a demon in the shape of a bear that ate small children, and _bugaboo_ may be an alteration of it. Puck and his cousins, Irish _púca_ and Cornish _bucca_, are often counted as kin too, though the exact relations can't be recovered.

The forms the Club uses are young. The OED records _bogey_ for the Devil from 1836, the "Old Bogey" of nursery speech, and _bogeyman_ from 1865. American spellings ran off on their own: _boogey man_ by 1898, _boogeyman_ by 1912.

## A threat without a shape

What sets the bogeyman apart from other monsters is that he has no description. Every household has its own, and many children never picture him at all. He is simply whatever is waiting if they don't behave. Folklorists call him a nursery bogey: a figure invoked almost entirely to control children, to send them to bed, keep them from the riverbank, or make them mind. The British folklorist Katharine Briggs filed the bogies and boggarts as a type of their own. The tale-type indexes give him no single number; the bogeyman is a role more than a story.

The shapelessness is the point. A monster with no fixed form fits any transgression and takes on whatever a particular child fears most. Local tellings give him claws at the window, glowing eyes, or horns, but those are additions to an empty center.

## Everywhere at bedtime

Nearly every culture keeps one, and the family resemblance is strong: he comes at night or at bedtime, he punishes disobedience, and very often he carries a sack. Spain and Latin America have the Coco, who watches from the roof, and the Sack Man, who was feared less as a creature than as a plausible kidnapper. Italy has the Babau, a tall man in a black coat and hood. Germany has der schwarze Mann and the Butzemann. Russia has Babay, an old man left undescribed on purpose so each child could fill him in. Bulgaria's Torbalan takes his name from his sack. On the Oga Peninsula in Japan, men in ogre masks and straw capes still go door to door at New Year asking whether there are any crying or lazy children in the house.

Some of these figures are bigger than the job. Baba Yaga, the Namahage, and La Llorona have full traditions of their own and are only partly bogeymen. The constants are the dark, the sack or the devouring mouth, and the bad behavior.

## The Enlightenment objects

Goya put the bogeyman on paper in 1799. Plate 3 of _Los Caprichos_, "Que viene el Coco" ("Here Comes the Bogey-Man"), shows a shrouded figure looming over a mother and two terrified children. The manuscript commentaries read it as a protest against "making a child more afraid of the Coco than of his father, and forcing him to fear what does not exist." The bogeyman, for Goya, was not a monster but a habit: child-rearing by invented dread.

The nineteenth century answered by industrializing the cautionary tale. Heinrich Hoffmann's _Struwwelpeter_ (1845) gave each vice its own punishment, and its great tall tailor who cuts off the thumbs of thumb-suckers is a domesticated bogeyman assigned to a single offense. By the 1880s the music halls had turned the figure into a comic song, and one of those Victorian "Bogie Man" numbers was most likely the one in Major Wellman's head when he named golf's phantom.

## The golfers' borrowing

That is the inheritance the golfers took. They didn't need a monster. They needed a name for an opponent who was never seen and never missed, one who was somehow always already there at the end of the hole. The nursery had spent centuries refining exactly that figure, and Wellman's remark that the ground score was "a regular bogey-man" landed, one suspects, because every Victorian golfer had grown up with him.

Golf then did to the word what _Struwwelpeter_ had done to the figure: it domesticated him. The phantom got a membership, a rank, and in time a march. The next century gave the bogeyman his other modern jobs, as the aviator's unidentified contact and the politician's manufactured threat, but only golf made him a fellow member.

Read this way, the word keeps both halves. The Colonel is the opponent, steady and exact. The bogeyman is the older thing underneath him: the shapeless dread on the first tee that a golfer brings from home and fills in privately.
