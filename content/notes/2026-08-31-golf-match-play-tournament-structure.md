A match-play tournament is usually a bracket tournament built out of individual match-play rounds.

The simplest form looks like the NCAA basketball tournament or a tennis draw. Suppose 64 golfers qualify. In the first round there are 32 head-to-head matches. The 32 winners advance, then 16, then 8, then 4, then 2, until one player wins the championship.

Each match is scored independently, hole by hole. So a player's tournament path might look like:

First round: won 4 & 3  
Round of 32: won 1 up  
Round of 16: won 2 & 1  
Quarterfinal: won in 20 holes  
Semifinal: won 5 & 4  
Final: won 3 & 2

There is no cumulative score. Winning 8 & 7 in one round gives you no advantage in the next round over someone who survived in 21 holes. Both simply advance.

Many tournaments begin with stroke-play qualifying. A common format is something like this: 100 or more players play one or two rounds of stroke play; the lowest 64 scores qualify for the match-play bracket; those 64 are seeded according to their qualifying scores; then single-elimination match play determines the champion. The U.S. Amateur is a prominent example of this general structure.

Other tournaments use group or pool play before elimination rounds. Four golfers might be placed in a group and play three matches, one against each of the others. A win might be worth one point and a halved match half a point. The player with the best group record advances into a knockout bracket. The WGC-Dell Technologies Match Play used a version of this format during its later years.

Team match-play tournaments work differently again. The Ryder Cup is the familiar example. Individual matches contribute points to an overall team score:

Win a match = 1 point  
Halve a match = ½ point for each team  
Lose = 0 points

The individual match can still finish 4 & 3 or 2 & 1, but that margin does not affect the team standings. A 7 & 6 victory and a 1-up victory are each worth exactly one point.

There is also an important distinction between individual match play and paired formats. In singles, one golfer plays another. In four-ball, two-player teams compete, with each golfer playing their own ball and the team's better score counting on each hole. In foursomes, two-player teams alternate shots using one ball. All three can be scored as match play.

The fundamental structure is therefore:

Hole → match → tournament.

You win holes to win a match. You win matches to advance through a bracket or earn points. The actual stroke totals largely disappear once they have determined who won each hole.

That is one of the attractions of match play: the tournament becomes a sequence of direct contests rather than a four-day comparison against an entire field.
